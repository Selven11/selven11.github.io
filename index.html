05

A walking stick that missed the stairs

A friend and I built a cane for people with low vision, using a micro:bit and ultrasonic sensors. We tested it near a staircase, and it missed the drop. Sensors pointed forward cannot see a step down.

What broke, and why

  1. Night lightFour LEDs in series off four batteries. One LED died and the whole string went dark.In series, one open LED opens the loop. The fix was groups of 2, 2 and 3 in parallel, off one 12 V battery.
  2. Walking stickForward-facing ultrasonic sensors missed the drop at a staircase.A sensor pointed forward can't see a step down.
  3. Rotating TVAt Nationals the gear chewed the boxes instead of turning over them.A part came loose.
  4. AeroWattAveraged data said the drone was six times more sensitive to heat than it was.Averaging hid where the windows didn't line up. Aligning the traces caught it.
  5. ROV boardThe first regulator I picked needs 18 V or more. The bench supplies 12 V.So it's an LM61460 buck, which runs from 12 V.

I measure power on a drone's flight computer, and I'm designing a power board for an underwater robot.

AeroWatt

Stevens Institute of Technology, summer 2026. Six INA226 current monitors on the flight computer, calibrated against a Monsoon to 1.07% mean agreement. I ran 54 trials, more than 48 million samples, and caught an averaging artifact that overstated thermal sensitivity sixfold. Second of nine authors, accepted at IEEE MILCOM 2026.

Purdue ROV

Electrical team, X-19 test bench power board. 12 V in, a buck to 5 V for the Pi, switched 12 V for the thrusters, and four INA237 monitors reporting over CAN. I chose the LM61460 because the first regulator I considered needs 18 V or more. I'm designing it now.

Schematic

The ROV power board, sheet by sheet

Four sheets in KiCad for the X-19 test bench. Pick one and it draws itself. Click the sheet to zoom in.

12 V comes in on an XT60, a barrel jack or USB-C. An AP33772S asks the USB-C charger for 12 V. LM74700 ideal diodes pick the live source and block reverse current, an SMBJ15A clamps spikes, and an LM5069 hot-swap controller limits inrush and cuts out over 16.2 V.

An LM61460 buck makes 5 V for the Raspberry Pi. I picked it because the first regulator I looked at needs 18 V or more and can't run from 12 V. A MIC5504 drops 5 V to 3.3 V for the logic.

Four INA237 monitors measure current and voltage on the rails, and a TMP1075 watches board temperature. Same idea as AeroWatt, on a board that swims.

An STM32 reads the monitors over I2C and reports on the ROV's CAN bus through a TCAN1044 transceiver, so the control station sees every rail.
